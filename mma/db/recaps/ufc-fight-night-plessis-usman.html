Summary

The UFC's return to Oklahoma City was a pretty good card: the fights delivered, there were some genuinely good performances, and you could hear the excitement of the crowd all night. The two biggest issues had nothing to do with the action: the lack of promotion and the lack of stars. This event flew so far under the radar that most casual fans probably didn't know it happened, which is a shame, because the Paycom Center crowd got their money's worth.


In the main event, Dricus du Plessis did exactly what he needed to do, battering Kamaru Usman for five rounds to sweep the scorecards. But let's pump the brakes on the celebration: Usman just isn't the fighter he used to be, so the win is not as impressive as some people are making it out to be. Usman looked absolutely terrible, and it's a miracle he wasn't finished in the first two rounds. The idea that this man was calling for a title shot last year is absolutely insane.


The card also highlighted the annoying place the UFC is in right now: they refuse to commit to number one contender fights, so we get matchups that look good on paper but leave it completely unclear what comes next. Elsewhere on the card, Fatima Kline bulldozed through Tabatha Ricci and earned herself a top-5 opponent, Tommy McMillen walked Alberto Montes down and beat him to a pulp for a third-round finish while looking sloppy doing it, Chase Hooper saved his career with a first-round submission, and Christian Leroy Duncan got a dull decision over Jared Cannonier in a fight that made it clear the 42-year-old has one fight left in him.


Final Score: 7.2/10



Main Card

Dricus du Plessis vs. Kamaru Usman

Result: Decision (unanimous) (50–45, 49–46, 49–46) at R5 5:00

The size difference everyone worried about at the faceoffs turned out to be exactly the story of the fight. Dricus du Plessis started chopping at Kamaru Usman with calf kicks early, hurt him with a big right hand, and completely shucked off Usman's first single leg attempt like it was nothing. Usman managed a body lock against the cage and landed the occasional jab and body kick, but even round one came with a momentum-killing low blow from Usman that briefly stopped the action.


The second round is where this became a beating. Du Plessis wobbled Usman with a knee to the head, wobbled him again with a combination, landed a head kick, and kept feeding him knees to the head every time he ducked in on a takedown. Then came the moment that should have cost Usman a point: after eating a big right hook, Usman complained about an eye poke that never happened, and the referee stopped the action. It completely killed the momentum of the exchange, and du Plessis could have ended the fight right there. DDP smelled blood after the restart and smothered Usman with knees up the middle and another head kick, but the window had closed. From the third round on, Usman went timid, fighting entirely off the back foot while du Plessis tagged him at range. The fourth brought a head kick that wobbled Usman so badly he literally ran away, and the fifth brought another huge head kick that had him wobbling again. Usman's only bright spots were a couple of right hooks that stung du Plessis late. The scorecards read 50-45 and 49-46 twice, and none of them were controversial.


Usman looked absolutely terrible out there. It's a miracle he wasn't finished in the first or second round, and it's absolutely insane that this man was calling for a title fight last year. He should not be anywhere near a title shot off that performance. As for du Plessis, he did his job and stays firmly in the title mix. Run Brendan Allen vs. DDP next.

Christian Leroy Duncan vs. Jared Cannonier

Result: Decision (unanimous) (30–27, 30–27, 29–28) at R3 5:00

This one went about how the odds said it would, just with less style than Christian Leroy Duncan would have liked. The first round was a grimy affair: an early low blow on Jared Cannonier stopped the action, and from there Cannonier did what Cannonier does, dragging the fight into the clinch against the fence. CLD landed a knee to Cannonier's head while he was holding onto a single leg, scored with elbows, and put Cannonier down briefly, and late in the round he hurt Cannonier badly and had him on the ropes, only to lose his balance attempting a spinning elbow and hit the canvas himself. Cannonier survived and closed the round with a takedown, holding the back at the bell.


Duncan got right back to work in the second, dropping Cannonier with a body kick and tagging him with what was most likely an illegal knee to the head. Cannonier kept answering the only way he could: single legs, wearing on Duncan in the clinch, an elbow on the break, and a couple of fence grabs he got away with while CLD landed ground and pound from the back. There were flashes of a real fight in there, Duncan landing a spinning elbow and Jared waving him forward to bring it on, but the third round told the story: CLD used kicks to keep distance and stumbled Cannonier with 1-2s, while Cannonier's takedowns did nothing because Duncan popped right back up every single time.


A pretty dull fight, but that's what Cannonier does. He isn't as athletically gifted as the other fighters, so he has to slow everything down with the clinch against the fence, and he did. The bigger concern is that Duncan did not look good out there: he was clearly gassing, couldn't have his way with a 42-year-old, and spent stretches of the fight just surviving. As for Cannonier, he has one fight left in him. Give him a fight in Arizona so he can retire in his home state. The controversial Imavov knockout was the fight that ended his career, and everything since has been the epilogue.

Chase Hooper vs. Mitch Ramirez

Result: Submission (rear-naked choke) at R1 2:15

Mitch Ramirez came out aggressive, taking the center of the octagon and moving forward like a man who intended to make this a fight. That lasted about as long as it took Chase Hooper to shoot a takedown and get it to the ground. From there the script wrote itself: Hooper transitioned to the back, sunk in the body triangle, and settled into a stalemate where he was mostly just stalling. He spent a while fishing for a rear naked choke with one arm before finally transitioning to a real rear naked choke and getting the tap at 2:15 of the first.


Cut Mitch Ramirez. Dude is fucking shit. Two winless UFC appearances and now getting submitted inside a round by a guy on a two-fight skid: there is nothing left to evaluate here.


For Hooper, that was a career-saving victory, plain and simple. But let's not confuse survival with progress: he still isn't any good, and he will get slimed out by any reasonable striker in the lightweight division. The move is to keep giving Hooper scrubs to work on his striking before throwing him back at a ranked opponent, or anyone near the rankings, again.

Fatima Kline vs. Tabatha Ricci

Result: Decision (unanimous) (30–27, 30–27, 30–27) at R3 5:00

So much for the wake up call. Fatima Kline answered every question about her level of competition by bulldozing through a top-10 opponent for fifteen minutes. The first round set the tone: Tabatha Ricci shot her takedowns and Kline denied them against the fence, all while looking sharp on the feet with a 1-2 into a head kick. Then Kline flipped the script entirely, hitting a double leg of her own against the cage, taking Ricci's back, and leaving the grappler swinging small punches off her back just to stay busy.


Ricci had one moment in the second, catching a Kline head kick and turning it into a takedown, but Kline popped right back up and went back to dominating the wrestling exchanges: sprawling on Ricci's next attempt and taking her back again, then nearly ending it with a rear naked choke that only the bell saved Ricci from. By the third, Kline was just showing off, slamming Ricci to the canvas multiple times with the body lock and voluntarily letting her back up to do it again. All three judges scored it 30-27.


Kline bulldozed through Ricci, full stop, and beating the No. 8 strawweight at her own grappling game is the kind of statement that demands a real test. Give Kline a top-5 opponent next. As for Ricci, she isn't even that old, but she got thoroughly outclassed tonight. She should take some time and bounce back with a more favorable matchup.

Tommy McMillen vs. Alberto Montes

Result: TKO (punches) at R3 3:29

Tommy McMillen came out aggressive off the opening bell and never stopped walking forward for three rounds. That is both the good news and the bad news. The first round was sloppy on both ends: McMillen tripped Alberto Montes to the ground, got nothing done there, and let him back up, and meanwhile Montes was clipping McMillen consistently every time he surged into the pocket. At one point Montes stunned him with a jab so cleanly that McMillen bounced off the canvas. McMillen answered by piecing Montes up against the fence, then got stung on the counter again for his trouble.


The second round was where the insane pace started to pay off. McMillen just walked Montes down relentlessly, and while Montes had his counters and a knee up the middle, McMillen was winning the exchanges and dug a nasty body shot against the cage. In the third, the dam broke: an uppercut and a body shot put Montes down, McMillen landed some ground and pound before letting him back up, and then the battering started in earnest. Body shots, a right hook, middle fingers from McMillen mid-fight, and Montes on the retreat just getting fucked up on the feet, going down and getting back up until the referee finally stopped it at 3:29 of round three.


McMillen gets the finish. Good for him. He went out there, walked Montes down, and beat him to a pulp. But he is super sloppy: he was getting clipped by Montes on the counter all night, and a good fighter would eat this dude for breakfast. The playbook stays the same: keep giving him "favorable," "winnable" matchups so he learns to use his reach advantage and stop getting hit so much. As for Montes, there isn't much stock loss here. He took on an unranked up-and-comer, nearly went the distance, and just needs some time off to get his mojo back.

Prelims

Jose Miguel Delgado vs. Austin Bashi

Result: Decision (unanimous) (30–27, 29–28, 29–28) at R3 5:00

The DWCS Season 8 grudge match delivered a real fight, even if it exposed Austin Bashi in the process. Bashi actually started well, cracking Jose Miguel Delgado early and sending him down before smothering him with takedown attempts, and later stunning him with another right hand. But Delgado kept using the fence to stay upright, kept popping back up from Bashi's slams, and slowly took over the striking. By the end of the first, Delgado was cracking Bashi on his takedown entries, kneeing him badly in the head as he stood up, and pouring on nasty ground and pound from the back to close the round. How Delgado didn't finish the fight in that first round is genuinely hard to understand.


The pace slowed significantly from there, and the fight settled into a pattern: Bashi chasing single legs and occasionally landing a slam or taking the back, Delgado sprawling, standing back up, and piecing Bashi up at range with 1-2s, step-in knees, and body kicks. Bashi hunted guillotines in the third and finished the fight landing ground and pound from half guard, but the damage ledger clearly favored Delgado, and the judges agreed.


The takeaway is simple: Bashi is not big enough for featherweight. Delgado had a reach advantage, and that was basically all she wrote, because Bashi could not compete with it. His striking isn't very good either: he doesn't pick shots or put together real combinations, he just spams kicks and punches without much strategy. Delgado moves on as the best featherweight product of DWCS Season 8, and Bashi has some structural problems to solve.

Jean-Paul Lebosnoyani vs. Ko Seok-hyeon

Result: Decision (unanimous) (29–28, 29–28, 29–28) at R3 5:00

The battle of unbeaten UFC starts turned out to be exactly as competitive as advertised. Ko Seok-hyeon opened with a takedown attempt that Jean-Paul Lebosnoyani nearly turned into a guillotine, and after Ko survived and worked free, the two settled into trading on the feet. The second round was the best of the fight: both guys came out swinging for the bleachers, Ko got the fight to the ground and landed ground and pound from inside Lebosnoyani's guard against the cage, and then the momentum flipped when Ko started to fade. Lebosnoyani hit a takedown in the center, passed to side control, took the back, and even worked into mount as Ko scrambled underneath him. The third was more trading in the center, a body kick from Lebosnoyani, and a late takedown that Ko popped right back up from.


A close fight. Both guys had their moments, but neither ever really distanced himself from the other: whenever one got an edge, the other came roaring back. The judges gave it to Lebosnoyani 29-28 across the board, and his unbeaten UFC run continues while Ko takes his first promotional loss.

Felipe Franco vs. Levi Rodrigues Jr.

Result: TKO (punches) at R2 1:40

So much for "Baby Monster." The first round was what everyone hoped for on paper: two Brazilian finishers trading bombs on the feet. But Felipe Franco found the smarter path late in the round, getting his grappling going and landing ground and pound before the bell. In the second, he went right back to it, worked his way into mount up against the cage, and started dropping bombs on Levi Rodrigues Jr. until the referee stepped in at 1:40.


There was a lot of controversy about whether the stoppage was good or not. Forreal though, what did you think was going to happen? Dude was in mount up against the cage, meaning he couldn't shrimp away or get separation, and bombs were getting dropped on him. That fight was over.


The bigger lesson is about the Contender Series pipeline: it is super hard to gauge whether these guys are any good. They typically get matched with someone at their level on the show, and then get humbled the moment they debut in the UFC. Rodrigues came in with six first-round finishes and left with the "regional highlight reel" question answered, at least for now. Good bounce-back win for Franco in his return to 205.

Ezra Elliott vs. Damien Anderson

Result: Decision (unanimous) (30–27, 29–28, 29–28) at R3 5:00

The four-days-notice special turned into fifteen minutes of chain wrestling. Ezra Elliott controlled the center from the start and committed fully to the takedown game, eating a clean shot on his first single leg entry but using the fence to stay upright and finishing the takedown anyway. The entire fight followed that template: Elliott dragging Damien Anderson down, Anderson bouncing right back up, and Elliott refusing to give up on the next attempt. He took the back in the first but couldn't find the position for a rear naked choke, threatened a d'arce from top position in the second but just couldn't get the positioning to finish it, and spent the third doing the same thing: chaining wrestling together and wearing on Anderson. Anderson had his moments, sprawling on shots, landing an elbow from the back, and taking a round on one card (albeit a controversial one).


Maybe we should be a bit more gracious with Elliott here: he took the fight this week and had a bad weight cut. It would have been nice to see more damage or a finish, but he clearly puts the grappling together well and has the cardio to do it for three rounds at least. The glaring hole is control: he cannot keep letting opponents stand up so easily. If you spend all that energy getting a guy down, you should keep him there.


And Anderson is probably better than we expect. He got back to his feet over and over against a grappler with a much stronger wrestling pedigree, while fighting up a division on the same short notice. Give both of these guys a fight with a full camp.

Alden Coria vs. Stewart Nicoll

Result: Decision (unanimous) (30–27, 30–27, 29–28) at R3 5:00

This was supposed to be the coronation prelim for Alden Coria, and instead it was a slog. The first round looked like the script: Stewart Nicoll bounced around the octagon basically refusing to engage, and Coria tagged him with a right hand, closed the distance, and landed jabs at will. But then Nicoll started wrestling. He finished a takedown (with what looked from the camera angle like a knee to the head of a grounded Coria mixed in), landed a nice combination, and kept shooting through the end of the round.


The second is where things got genuinely concerning. After a low blow from Coria, Nicoll started leading the dance on the feet and landing significantly more strikes, and when he got reversed in a scramble, he answered by lifting Coria up and slamming him in the center of the octagon. Coria salvaged the round by taking the back and riding out position, but the third was more of the same weirdness: Nicoll got a takedown in the center and Coria responded by scooting his own butt to the fence. What the fuck? Coria spent the rest of the fight jumping guillotine after guillotine while Nicoll wore on him against the cage and stalled, and none of the chokes came close enough to matter.


A pretty pathetic performance from Coria. Nicoll is truly a bottom of the barrel UFC caliber fighter, and to not only go to a decision with him but actually drop a round to him is pathetic. That dude has to go back and work on a lot of things: cardio, grappling, striking, the works. The top-15 talk should be shelved indefinitely. And Nicoll? He sucks. He's now 0-4 in the UFC. Cut him.

RJ Harris vs. Alvin Hines

Result: TKO (punches) at R1 1:40

The all-American heavyweight scrap lasted exactly 100 seconds and told us everything we needed to know. Alvin Hines came out spamming low kicks, RJ Harris answered with a left hook and a straight right, and both of these guys looked extremely slow and lethargic doing it. Then Harris uncorked an uppercut that put Hines down on the canvas, and Hines took an insane amount of punishment before the fight was finally stopped at 1:40.


Hines looked terrible out there, and it fits the pattern: athletes, fighters in general, do not come back the same once they fail a drug test and get suspended, and this was nothing different. With two straight losses to start his UFC career and a PED suspension on his record, it's time to get rid of him. The UFC shouldn't tolerate this crap.


Good win from Harris in his debut, moving him to 6-0. But let's be honest about what we watched: this fight was another exhibit in the case that heavyweight is an excuse of a weight class for guys who don't want to cut weight before fighting.

Dione Barbosa vs. Anna Melisano

Result: Submission (rear-naked choke) at R1 4:04

This was just a mismatch from the opening minute. Dione Barbosa shot a takedown early, worked the over-unders against the cage while Anna Melisano used the fence to stay upright, and once she finally finished the takedown, the judo pedigree took over completely. Barbosa transitioned to the back and threatened a rear naked choke, moved through half guard, landed ground and pound from side control, then got back to the back and cranked on a neck crank that Melisano somehow survived. It only delayed the inevitable: Melisano got back to her feet with Barbosa still glued to her back, and the rear naked choke sank in at 4:04 of the first.


Full credit where it's due on the pick: more research should have gone into this fight before picking Melisano. She took the fight on short notice, and it showed. She had no business being in there with Barbosa, and the grappling gap was apparent from the very first clinch. Barbosa moves to 4-2 in the UFC and remains a nightmare for anyone who can't stop her trips, while Melisano goes back to the drawing board after a debut that came too soon.
